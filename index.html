How I lead people through a change

AI adoption is a change problem before it is a technology problem, so this comes first. These three systems are how I bring an operation and its people from where they are to a new way of working. I built them running recruiting at an industrial distributor and people operations at a nonprofit, years before generative AI, and wrote them down for a hiring manager in 2026 with a cake on every step, because a good process should make sense anywhere.

first ninety days

The Entry Sequence

How I take on a team or a problem: understand it from the inside before changing it, then earn the capacity to do the real work.

  1. Learn the work myself
  2. Shadow and listen
  3. Weigh cost, benefit, risk
  4. Harvest low hanging fruit
  5. Reinvest the capacity

Stabilize, reclaim capacity, then spend it where it matters most.

how change sticks

The Rollout Loop

How a new way of working survives contact with the people who have to do it.

  1. Prototype with trusted hands
  2. Make it safe to try
  3. Let ambassadors carry it
  4. Roll out with support
  5. Checkpoint on the calendar

Revise, then run the loop again. The process expects it.

how people come along

Designed Participation

How I get people to join something voluntarily, which is the only way adoption ever lasts.

  1. Diagnose what motivates them
  2. Lower the cost of entry
  3. Open multiple modes
  4. Codify what works

When people opt out, I redesign the structure before I push harder.

The Trust Ladder below is the same thinking pointed at a tool instead of a team. The Rollout Loop asks whether a change has earned its place in the work; the ladder asks whether an AI output has earned its place in the decision. When I introduce AI to a group, I run both at once: the ladder for the work, Designed Participation for the people.

The Trust Ladder

Most people decide how much to trust AI output on feel. I decided to make it a sequence. Each rung is a question I answer before moving to the next, and every piece of work on this page went up the same five rungs. It is also what I teach: adults adopt a tool when they can see where their own judgment still lives in the process, and the ladder shows them exactly where.

1.

Frame

Start from the operating problem and its constraints, never from the deliverable. The tool cannot tell me what matters; I have to say it first.

My weekly job search began as "find six to ten live roles that pass six specific filters," not "write me a prompt."

2.

Brief

Write the context down once so it does not have to be retaught: the brand rules, the voice rules, the spec, the handoff file.

A two sheet design system that every generated document must follow, read before anything gets built.

3.

Check

Verify the output against the thing it claims to describe, not against how good it sounds.

A hiring checklist put the reference check in the wrong place. The draft read fine. The real process said otherwise.

4.

Decide

Keep what earned it, reject what did not, switch tools when execution falls short of the analysis, and write the lesson down as a rule.

After one tool gave excellent feedback and a weak resume, I moved production to another and kept seven rules from the failure.

5.

Hand off

Turn the one time task into something that runs without me, then into something other people run.

A Monday job sweep that emails me a shortlist. Students drafting resumes before appointments. An Executive Director reviewing a handbook with AI on her own.

2025 · Early childhood education nonprofit · Senior Manager of People and Culture

How AI spread through a small nonprofit

I joined a Chicago nonprofit to run its people operations and found hiring that depended on memory and goodwill. I did not run a workshop on AI. I used it to produce things the team needed, let the results do the persuading, and watched the practice travel further than I had planned. By the time I left, the Executive Director was using AI herself to review the employee handbook.

What I did

  • Diagnosed the operational problem and chose where to start
  • Supplied the real hiring stages, competencies, and tracking
  • Caught the reference check error and set the correction standard
  • Decided to hand over finished outputs rather than ask for prompting
  • Encouraged and supported wider use over the following months

What AI did

  • Drafted the hiring checklist from my stages
  • Generated competency based question banks per opening
  • Helped design the Slack workflow forms for candidate status
How it went up the ladder
Frame

The problem was consistency: every hiring manager interviewed differently, and steps were skipped because nothing wrote them down. The goal was a process people would actually follow, not a policy.

Brief

I gave the tool our real stages, the competencies for each role, and the tracking we already used, then generated a hiring checklist, Slack workflow forms for candidate status, and competency based interview question banks for each opening.

Check

The first checklist read cleanly and was wrong. It placed reference checks after the offer decision. In our process, references are requested before the final interview and checked after it, before any offer. I corrected the sequence against how we actually hired, and that correction became the standard for everything after it.

Decide

I handed managers finished question banks rather than asking them to generate their own. In Designed Participation terms, that is lowering the cost of entry: the first experience with the new workflow asked nothing of them but to use it. The Rollout Loop's "make it safe to try" is the same instinct.

Hand off

Once the hiring tools were in use, I started encouraging people to bring AI to other tasks they were already doing, one at a time. By the time I left, the Executive Director was using it herself to review the employee handbook. I cannot claim I caused that, but I can describe what came before it.

What I would teach from this

Adoption is a trust problem before it is a training problem. Start with useful work, show the process, fix the first error where people can see it, and make independent experimenting easy. In this case, the most senior person in the building picked it up without being asked.

July to October 2026 · Your Career Mentor · About nineteen versions, built with AI

The Career Fit Tool: two worksheets become a product

A free, self guided web tool that walks a job seeker or student through which industries draw them, which work environments suit them, and which strengths they are both good at and actually enjoy, then builds a personal Career Fit Map. It takes 25 to 35 minutes, saves in the browser, and sends a styled summary to the person and to me. It started as two worksheets I had written by hand for my coaching program. I wrote the content. AI wrote the code. I was the product owner, designer, tester, and editor through roughly nineteen versions.

What I did

  • Wrote the source content: the industries guide and the 72 skill strengths assessment were my coaching worksheets
  • Owned scope, wording, data, and privacy decisions through every version
  • Tested each version live, reported what broke or felt wrong, and decided what shipped
  • Chose the off the shelf stack: WordPress and Elementor, a Zapier webhook, Google Sheets, Gmail, and ruled out three alternatives

What AI did

  • Wrote the code: one self contained HTML, CSS, and JavaScript file pasted into my site
  • Drafted one of the twelve industry profiles at my request, which I approved
  • Ran regression tests on every version, checked that all 71 work verbs resolve to a skill category, and checked every line of copy for em dashes
How it went up the ladder
Frame

My program front loaded weeks two and three with homework: a long industries reading, a strengths self assessment, employer research worksheets. Clients lost momentum right where momentum is most fragile. At my day job, one person now covers work four people used to hold. I needed something that gathers a person's answers before we meet, so the first conversation starts at the interesting part.

Brief

The worksheets were the brief. I gave the tool the industry profiles and the skills list I had already written, the four part structure, and the rules: everything saves in the browser, nothing is asked for until the end, and no job titles in the public tool, because titles are the part people come to me for.

Check

Every version was tested on the live site, and the live site kept disagreeing with the draft. Buttons worked but gave no confirmation, so I had visible status added to every action. The site theme overrode the tool's styles and turned thin bars into solid orange blocks, so every rule was scoped to the tool and verified against the live theme. Results arrived as attachments, so they were changed to render inside the email. A 270 KB live file was updated with a twelve edit diff and a checksum rather than re-pasting the whole thing.

Decide

The judgment calls are where the behavioral science lives. ESG became a cross cutting lane rather than a thirteenth industry, because ESG people work inside banks, utilities, and manufacturers. Lanes are ranked by where their funding concentrates in the industries a person liked, because raw match counts put the same two lanes at the top for everyone. A "Worth Reconsidering" section flags one dismissed industry per match, because listing every overlap tested as noise. A guided one at a time view and an all at once view both exist, because people differ. "Haven't used this enough to know" is an answer, because guessing produces bad data. Privacy was written down before anything was sold: name and email only at the end, results in a sheet only I open, cohort reports never name a student, and a fictional school for the demo so nothing reads as an official partnership.

Hand off

It replaced two weeks of homework in my program and has been the first step for paying clients since August 2026. An institutional version tags every student from a given school and matches their results to that campus's own programs, with a "hidden gem" tag on underused ones. It is the centerpiece of what I bring to small and under-resourced career centers, and the discovery check I would use to measure it is drafted, not built, with every figure to be reported against the number who answered.

What I would teach from this

You can turn what you already know into a working tool without a developer or a budget, if you treat the AI as the builder and yourself as the product owner. Keep the content, the judgment, and the privacy decisions yours, test on the real thing rather than the draft, and write down what you will not do before anyone asks you to do it.

October 2026 · Your Career Mentor · A template, instantiated per client

The Story Deck: interview practice as a TV guide

Clients knew their stories and still rambled in interviews, because they started talking before they had picked which story to tell. So I built practice around that one failure. The Story Deck is a web page shaped like a TV guide: a highlight reel of the client's wins, a rehearsal mode where a question comes up as a card, a clock runs with a green sweet spot between 1:15 and 2:30, and the client answers out loud before flipping the card to the script. Reshoot brings a question back sooner; That's a wrap retires it. Everything stays in the browser. The version here is the template, built for a fictional client named Mary.

What I did

  • Diagnosed the failure the practice had to fix: talking before choosing
  • Designed the format, the two storylines, the runtime zones, and the card structure
  • Wrote the question set and the script scaffolds, with placeholders where the client's own detail goes
  • Tested it with clients and revised

What AI did

  • Wrote the code for the page, the clock, the card flip, the browser-only practice log, and the optional transcription
  • Drafted the fictional sample client's stories for the template
How it went up the ladder
Frame

The problem was not a lack of stories. Clients had the stories and the scripts. The problem was the moment between hearing the question and opening their mouth, and no document fixes that. Only practice does.

Brief

I specified the learning mechanics first: answer out loud without looking, compare to the script afterward, get timing feedback while talking, and see the hard questions again sooner than the easy ones. Then the frame: a TV guide, because the client is the main character and the hiring manager wants to watch an episode.

Check

The test was whether a client could use it alone between sessions without me explaining it. The instructions, the key shortcuts, and the glossary of terms exist because the first version assumed too much.

Decide

Three decisions carry the behavioral science. Recall before review, because telling it from memory is where it sticks. A visible runtime with a green zone, because people cannot feel two minutes while talking. Reshoot and wrap, so the deck spaces the questions a person struggles with instead of cycling through everything evenly. Every take stays on the client's computer; the only thing they bring me is what they choose to.

Hand off

It is a template. Each client gets their own deck with their highlight reel, their questions, and their scripts, and a call sheet for the day of. I build the content; the page does the practice.

What I would teach from this

Content is not practice. If the failure happens in the moment of performance, design the rehearsal around that moment, make the feedback visible while it is happening, and let the tool decide what to repeat.

October 2026 · My own job search

The resume that taught me to grade the tool

One AI assistant gave excellent analysis of a role and built a weak resume from it. A second gave thinner feedback and a better document. I told the first one so in plain words, asked it to diagnose the gap, kept seven production rules from the failure, and moved document production to the second tool for good.

What I did

  • Set the brief and the no invention rule for both tools
  • Read both documents as the recruiter would and graded them against the analysis
  • Named the gap, asked for a diagnosis, kept the rules, rerouted the work

What AI did

  • Tool A: the role analysis, a weak resume, the diagnosis, and the handoff file
  • Tool B: the resume that shipped
How it went up the ladder
Frame

The job was a two page resume that passes a skeptical recruiter's read, with every claim traceable to my master resume. Good advice was not the deliverable.

Brief

Both tools had the same inputs: the posting, the master resume, my formatting rules, and my standing instruction never to invent or inflate a figure.

Check

I read both documents the way the recruiter would, side by side, and compared each against the analysis I had already accepted. One matched its own advice. One did not.

Decide

I told the first tool its feedback was great and its execution was much worse, and asked it to diagnose the gap. Seven production rules came out of that, including the one I now apply everywhere: the finished artifact has to match the quality of the analysis that preceded it, or the analysis was not finished.

Hand off

I had the first tool write a handoff file carrying the analysis, the revisions, and the new rules, and moved document production to the second tool permanently.

What I would teach from this

A tool that explains well has not proven it can build well. Grade the artifact, not the conversation. This is the first exercise I would run with any team: same brief, two tools, grade the outputs blind.

July 2026 · Interviewing for a senior manager role at a large industrial supply company

The one page that explains how I lead

Between a recruiter screen and a hiring manager interview, I turned ten years of operating habits into the page you read above: three named practices, a cake on every step, results and principles along the bottom, built for one reader in her company's colors. I dictated ten stories, AI scripted them, and every number was marked either on record (it matched the documents I had submitted) or flagged (from memory, verify before saying it aloud). The reconciliation caught drift between resume versions and one place where the tool produced a logo and presented it as mine. I was cut before the panel round. The page outlived the process.

How it went up the ladder
Frame

The recruiter had told me what the hiring manager cared about: strengthening operations first, building capacity, then automating, before adding programs. I needed to show her how I do that, in a form she could hold, in under a week.

Brief

I dictated ten stories the way I would tell them aloud, gave the tool the resume and cover letter I had actually submitted, and set two rules: every number gets marked on record or flagged, and nothing gets invented to fill a gap.

Check

The reconciliation caught real drift: figures that had shifted between resume versions, a team structure described two ways, and a logo the tool had produced and presented as mine. I replaced it with the real asset and wrote a standing rule that day: never fabricate a branding asset and present it as preexisting. Ask first.

Decide

The frameworks already existed in how I worked. What the tool contributed was the discipline of naming them, sequencing them, and finding the one analogy that let a stranger follow all three in a minute.

Hand off

The three practices are now the How I lead section of this site, the ten stories are the bank I pull from in interviews, and the on record and flag markers became how I check every application document.

What I would teach from this

Make the tool show its sources. A two mark system, verified or not, turns AI drafting from a liability into a discipline, and it is the first habit I would give anyone who writes about their own work with these tools.

August 2026 · Personal · A web page that reads my calendars

A morning that reads the calendar before I do

The Morning Launchpad pulls today from my personal, business, and work calendars, decides which of three versions of the morning applies, and walks me through it: grounding, a timed brain dump, three priorities in a fixed order, and a threshold I cross before any inbox opens. The version I am proudest of is The Floor, for very early or very hard starts, where every step shrinks but none disappears. It has been my first screen most mornings since.

How it went up the ladder
Frame

The routine I knew worked only survived on days that cooperated. An early meeting, a rough night, or a full inbox would collapse it, and a routine that only works on good days is not a routine.

Brief

I wrote the routine down in three versions before any code existed, specified what the page should read from my calendars to choose between them, and what it should never do, which is send anything anywhere.

Check

I tested the smallest version on the mornings when the full routine was out of reach. If The Floor could get me from waking up to one named priority on a day I would otherwise have skipped everything, the design held. It did, often enough that I trust it.

Decide

What stayed was deliberately small: water and medication, a short grounding read aloud, a brain dump that archives itself, the Rule of Three (the essential job outcome, one thing for me, my business only if there is room), and a first message window set to a time of day rather than a feeling. This is Designed Participation turned on myself: design for variable capacity, and lower the cost of showing up.

Hand off

Underneath the routine sits a step tracker that breaks a larger plan into moves of thirty minutes or less, and a weekly mobility tracker that counts any day, consecutive or not, because streaks punish the people who need the habit most.

What I would teach from this

Design for the worst day, not the best one. A habit survives when its smallest version is still recognizably the habit, and when the tool does the remembering so the person does not have to.

Tools I built for myself

None of these were built for a client or an employer. Each one exists because something in my week kept costing attention, and an hour of building was cheaper than ten minutes a day of the problem.

every morningCoach's DeskReads my client board, six calendars, booking links, email, and meeting recordings, then writes one page: what needs a decision today, what the next nine days hold, and who is waiting on me. It tests live booking links rather than trusting them.
coachingResume CombinerBuilds a tagged inventory of a person's resume bullets by verb family, function, and quantified result, then matches a pasted job description to the strongest ones and lists the gaps.
operationsQuinn Reply BankA library of first replies for my operations manager, organized by the situation a message arrives in, so routine inbound gets a consistent, warm answer within the hour without me in the loop.
the businessMoney SplitA profit first calculator. Enter what came in and adjustable splits divide it into taxes, pay, operating, and profit, mapped to the real accounts the money moves to.
researchCross-Sector Title MapMaps the same work across sectors where it hides under different job titles. It is the private half of the Career Fit Tool: the tool shows functions, this holds the titles, and the titles stay with me.
thinkingIdeas NotebookA running notebook of observations and hypotheses where I use AI to find the competing explanation and the research tradition behind an idea before I decide what I think.

106 and Paige: a game show for one night

For my birthday I built a two screen party game: a host panel on my laptop and a separate audience display on the TV, twenty five dance crazes with clip times and trivia, team scoring, steals, and a lightning medley. The habit predates the tools

Generative AI is the newest chapter of a ten year pattern: find the repeated work, build the system, hand it to the people who do the work. The behavioral science underneath it is not decoration. Two years in a lab studying how people form impressions and make decisions is where I learned to treat adoption as a design problem rather than a willpower problem.

Process automationAs a product change supervisor at an industrial distributor, redesigned the team's workflow on a business process platform, taking more than a hundred weekly overtime hours to zero and lifting accuracy and efficiency by forty percent.
Recruiting inbox automationBuilt a tagging and routing system for a corporate recruiting inbox so that candidate questions reached the right person without a human sorting them.
A half million product pipelineWrote the SQL and Python that recategorized roughly 500,000 products out of a mainframe system, with the ground truth checks that made the output trustworthy.
Behavioral measurementTwo years as a research assistant in a social neuroscience lab studying how people form impressions and make decisions, and later a simulation based leadership assessment that measured how candidates handled conflict.
